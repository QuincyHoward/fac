Measurements of iron-plasma absorption spectrum over 150–1200 eV photon energy range were
reported at temperature T ¼ (72 6 4) eV. The electron temperature was diagnosed with the
absorption spectrum of aluminum mixed with iron. The density was not diagnosed directly but
obtained from a radiative hydrodynamic simulation with the Multi-1D code. The broad photon
energy range enables simultaneous observation of the L-shell and M-shell transitions that dominate
the radiation transport at this temperature. The spectrally resolved transmission data were
compared to the detailed-conﬁguration-accounting model calculations and reasonable agreement
was found. V

I. INTRODUCTION
Radiative opacity dominates the energy transport in high
temperature
plasmas
and
plays
an
important
role
in
researches such as astrophysics, inertial conﬁnement fusion
(ICF), and magnetic conﬁnement fusion (MCP).1 Theoretical
models of opacity are complex and rely on various approxi-
mations.2–6 To test these issues and approximations used in
the models, it is necessary to carry out opacity experiments
of hot dense plasmas. In the past twenty years, a series of
opacity experiments have been carried out on pulse high-
power machines such as laser and z-pinch.7–29 In 1990 s,
most opacity experiments were conducted on high power
laser facilities such as NOVA,7–11 HELEN,12,13 ASTERIX
IV,23,25 and OCTAL,17 and high quality data have been
obtained in some of these experiments. For example, Perry
et al. have measured the opacity of niobium in the several
keV photon energies, with the sample fully characterized by
independent electron temperature and density diagnosis
using the 1s-2p transition absorption spectrum of doped alu-
minum
tracer
and
x-ray
radiography,
respectively.7–9
Springer et al., however, have measured the opacity of iron
plasma in the x-ray ultraviolet (XUV) range at an electron
temperature of about 59 eV.10 Besides, Da Silva et al. have
measured the opacity of iron plasma at lower temperature
and demonstrated the importance of Dn ¼ 0 transition
absorption in the mean opacity of iron, which had not been
considered in early opacity models.11 Most of these laser-
based opacity experiments were conducted at relatively low
temperature. Since 2000, on the more powerful Z-pinch fa-
cility at Sandia National Laboratory, opacity experiments at
temperature above 150 eV have been conducted.20,21 The
high temperature opacity experiments enabled the ﬁrst direct
tests of opacities import in solar interior radiation transport.
Most measurements in previous work covered a relative
narrow photon energy range that included either the L-shell
absorption or the M-shell absorption. For example, the
Springer’s experiment addressed the photon energy range of
50–325 eV,10 and the Da Silva’s experiment covered a still
narrower energy range of 50–120 eV.11 The absorption in
these photon energies was dominated mainly by bound-to-
bound (b-b) and bound-to-free (b-f) M-shell transitions.
These experiments could provide data for opacity model
tests at low temperature, but the plasma conditions and tran-
sitions were very different from that occurring in stellar inte-
riors or inertial conﬁnement fusion capsule implosions.
Some of the other experiments have measured absorption
spectra over photon energy range above 700 eV by using a
Bragg crystal spectrometer and provided an opportunity to
study the absorption regime dominated mainly by the L-shell
b-b and b-f transitions of Mid-Z elements or the K-shell b-b
transitions of Low-Z elements. However, the relative low
temperature in these plasmas means that the L-shell transi-
tions in this range did not dominate the mean opacity and
thus the radiation transport.
Recently, a gated ﬂat-ﬁeld grating spectrometer that
covers a broad photon energy range from 150 eV to 1200 eV
has been developed for opacity experiments at the SGII laser
facility. The spectrometer exploits a 2400 lines/mm holo-
graphic ﬂat-ﬁeld grazing grating with low higher-order dif-
fractions and is coupled with a gated microchannel plate
(MCP) detector to record spectral signal during an 150 ps
period. The use of MCP detector is critical for suppressing
the self-emission with respect to the backlight in higher tem-
perature opacity experiments. Gold-plasma transmission has
been measured at the electron temperature Te  85 eV over
150–1200 eV with this spectrometer.29 The temperature was
much higher than in previous experiments ever conducted on
laser facilities, and the measurements covered the photon
energy range dominating the Rosseland mean opacity at
these temperatures. In addition, a target has also been devel-
oped for opacity experiments at the SGII laser facility, which
used a smaller cylindrical cavity with two foam-bafﬂes so
that both the stray laser light and wall plasmas can be pre-
vented more effectively from direct impinging on the sample
and driving it out of local thermodynamic equilibrium
(LTE). The improved target, together with the broad photon
energy coverage, and the time- and space-resolution of the

In this paper, we reported on the iron-plasma transmis-
sion measurement at electron temperature of about (72 6 4)
eV over a photon energy range from 150 eV to 1200 eV. The
experiments were conducted on the SGII laser facility with
an improved opacity target. The transmission spectrum of
the iron absorber was obtained from the measured backlight,
absorption, and self-emission spectra with a space-resolved
ﬂat-ﬁeld grating spectrometer. The backlight and absorption
spectra were measured simultaneously in a single shot, while
the self-emission spectrum was measured in another shot.
The temperature was deduced from the 1s-2p absorption
of the Al tracer doped in the sample, and the density of the
sample was determined by a 1-D radiative hydrodynamic sim-
ulation with the measured hohlraum radiation temperature.
The measured spectrally resolved transmission spectra were
compared with a detailed conﬁguration accounting (DCA)
opacity model, and their discrepancies were discussed. This
work has demonstrated the ability to perform opacity mea-
surement with high power laser facility to test opacity models
over a photon energy range that dominates the Rosseland
mean opacity at a higher temperature that is of interest for
astrophysics and inertial conﬁnement fusion research.
II. MEASUREMENTS
The x-ray radiation source required to produce a uni-
form plasma for absorption measurement was generated by
injecting the eight main beams of the SG-II laser facility into
a D-shaped gold cavity through two laser entrance holes
(LEHs) of 500 lm diameter, arranged in cones on either
side, so that each beam forms an angle of 45 to the cavity
axis. As is shown in Fig. 1, the target consists of two
D-shaped parts and a central cylindrical part, with the laser
lights hitting the wall of the D-shaped cavities and being
mostly converted into x-rays. The sample foil was placed in
the center of the cylindrical part and heated by the x-rays
uniformly but could not be irradiated by the laser lights,
either directly or indirectly. Moreover, the effect of plasma
ﬁlling on the x-ray absorption measurements can also be
avoided to a great extent using this target. The laser lights
were frequency-tripled and carried a total energy of about
2.0 kJ in 1.0 ns pulse duration. The temporal behavior of the
radiation source was monitored by a soft x-ray spectrometer
(SXS) from the laser entrance hole at 30 with respect to the
hohlraum axis.
The samples, consisting of a mixture of 75 lg/cm2 iron
and 40 lg/cm2 aluminum, were tamped on both sides by
50 lg/cm2 (or 0.5 lm in thickness) plastic layer. The Fe/Al
mixture was fabricated by plasma spraying of a Fe layer and
an Al layer. The Al layer attached to the Fe sample provides
an opportunity to characterize the Fe plasma by its k-shell
absorption line strengths, and this will be done in a separated
shot. The areal density of the samples was determined at the
Beijing Synchrocyclotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) by
comparing the measured transmission with the standard
transmission data, which was believed accurate to within a
few percent. This gives the iron areal density with uncer-
tainty of about 10%, including contributions from measure-
ment errors and the uncertainties of standard transmission
data.
The iron-plasma transmission was measured by the
method of absorption spectroscopy. The required uniform
backlight source in the experiment was produced by irradiat-
ing a gold foil with the beam-smoothed ninth laser light of
the SG-II laser facility. A gated ﬂat-ﬁeld grating spectrome-
ter exploiting a varied-line-spacing ﬂat-ﬁeld holographic gra-
ting with low higher-order diffractions was used to measure
the relevant spectra.30 This spectrometer covers the photon
energy range from 150 eV to 1200 eV and has a theoretical
spectral resolution k/Dk of higher than 1000. However, the
actual spectral resolution was about k/Dk  200, primarily
restricted by the limited spatial resolution of the MCP detec-
tor. The broad energy range means that this spectrometer is
able to cover the photon energy range dominating the Rosse-
land mean opacity at higher temperature than in prior work.
The time-resolution provided by the gated microchannel
plate detector was about 150 ps. The space-resolved spec-
trum consists of two spectral portions that represent, respec-
tively, the backlights either passing through the Fe/Al
sample or not, and is measured with the spectrometer using a
slit. The photon energies were calibrated by the absorption
or emission lines from carbon k-shell transitions. The space-
resolved absorption spectra measured in three separate shots
and a space-resolved self-emission spectrum in another shot
during the 1.7 ns period were shown in Fig. 2.

III. RADIATIVE HYDRODYNAMIC SIMULATIONS
OF PLASMA CONDITIONS
Radiative hydrodynamic simulation was used to deter-
mine the plasma conditions. The simulation uses the 1-D
version of the MULTI code developed by Ramis et al.31 The
sample foil to be simulated consists of a 0.1 lm-thick Fe
layer and a 0.15 lm-thick Al layer and is tamped on both
sides by two 0.5 lm-thick CH layers. The thermal x-ray radi-
ation heats the target from both sides of the sample foil. In
the simulation, the x-ray radiation ﬁeld at the sample was
measured from the laser entrance hole by a soft x-ray spec-
trometer consisting of seven ﬁltered x-ray diodes at an angle
of 30 with respect to the hohlraum axis. The temporal
behavior of the radiation source was shown in Fig. 4(a),
which showed a peak radiation temperature of about 105 eV
at 1.05 ns after laser irradiation.
In Fig. 4, the time history of the density and electron
temperature in the sample is plotted. It shows that the peak
temperature in the iron layer reaches about 100 eV at the
time t  1.15 ns after the beginning of the main laser pulse.
Thereafter, the temperature in the iron plasma tends to go
down. During the 1.65–1.75 ns period, when the transmis-
sion spectrum was probed, the temperature falls to about
90 eV, and the density fall to about 0.005 g/cm3. It can also
be seen from the ﬁgure that in the probe window (the shadow
region in Fig. 4(a)), the temperature and density changes
with time in the heated sample foil are quite small, with an
average error less than 2% for temperature and 15% for den-
sity. Besides, the modeled temperatures and densities at the
side and center regions of the sample show that the tempera-
ture variations with position during the probe period were
less than 5%, and the density variations in the sample were
no more than 20%. Besides, the electron temperatures in the
aluminum layer are about 4 eV lower than in iron layer dur-
ing the probe period.
The precision of the simulated density was determined
mainly by the input material equation of state and the radiation
source at the sample. Without considering the uncertainties
from EOS data, then the density is decided mostly by radiation
temperature, which is smaller than 2% as discussed above, or
corresponding to a density uncertainty of smaller than 6%.
In comparison with the temperature measured with the
Al data, the simulated temperature is much higher. The cause
of this difference was most likely ascribed to the fact that the
radiation temperature at the sample is lower than that meas-
ured from the laser entrance hole. A view factor analysis of
the illumination in the cavity showed that the radiation tem-
perature in the center of the hohlraum was more than 10%
lower than at the laser entrance hole, mainly due to the rela-
tive large diagnosing hole. In fact, by lowering the tempera-
ture of the radiation source from 105 eV to 90 eV, the
simulated temperature was lowered to about 80 eV (seen in

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS
The measured transmission was compared with the cal-
culations by a DCA opacity code. The DCA model assumes
LTE and calculate all the spectral quantities such as transi-
tion energy, transition intensity, and full width at half maxi-
mum (FWHM) of a transition array in a statistical approach
based on conﬁguration averaging.32 The transmission was
obtained from the calculated opacity lðvÞ by means of
Beer’s law (TðvÞ ¼ exp½lðvÞ  ql). The calculations use
the initial areal density of the sample, which was inferred
from the x-ray transmission measurements at the BSRF to be
about (85 6 10) lg/cm2. The Fe/Al transmissions were cal-
culated at temperatures and densities around the modeled
ones, with Al and Fe being treated independently and the
total transmission being obtained from the product of the Al
transmission and Fe transmission.

absorption features in the spectrum were due to the Fe
L-shell b-b transitions in the 700–1200 eV photon energy
range and to the M-shell transitions in the 150–350 eV. The
L-shell absorption feature near 800 eV were determined by

features in the higher photon energy range from 900–
1200 eV. These discrepancies may mostly due to the mea-
surement errors that originate in the weak backlight intensity
in this energy range. Besides, the approximate treatments of
the transition lines in the DCA model, the charge state distri-
bution changes due to the plasma nonuniformities, and the
shot to shot variations in the experiments may also contribute
to the discrepancies. The effect of self-emission on the trans-
mission was relatively small because the measured self-
emission is less than 10% of the backlight intensity. Particu-
larly, absorption lines from oxide K-shell transitions were
observed around 750 eV, indicating possible oxidation of the
sample, and this may also contribute to the discrepancies.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We have carried out spectrally resolved transmission
measurement of Fe plasma at temperature (72 6 4) eV and
over a broader energy range than in prior experiments ever
performed with high-power laser facilities. The transmission
was measured with a space-resolved and time-gated ﬂat-ﬁeld
grating spectrometer that covered an energy range from
150 eV to 1200 eV. The absorption spectrum of Al mixed
with Fe was used to infer the plasma temperature. The
plasma density was not diagnosed directly but obtained by
radiative hydrodynamic simulations with the measured hohl-
raum radiation temperature. The comparison showed that the
simulated temperature was much higher than the diagnosed
one, and this has been reasonably explained by a lower radia-
tion temperature at the sample than that measured directly
from the laser entrance hole. With the diagnosed temperature
and the density simulated with a lower radiation temperature
of 90 eV, the sample transmission was calculated by the
DCA opacity code. The measured transmission spectrum
was compared to the calculation, and as a whole the calcula-
tion agrees with the data quite well. The lack of structure in
the L-shell absorption of Fe has been ascribed to the relative
low spectral resolution of the spectrometer at higher ener-
gies. The experiment demonstrates a high temperature Fe-
plasma transmission measurement over a broad energy range
that covers both the L-shell and M-shell absorption regime,
with the self-emission being suppressed to less than 10% of
the backlight intensity with a gated detector. Other experi-
mental problems such as plasma uniformities, neglect of
higher order diffractions, nonuniformity of the MCP detec-
tor, areal density uncertainty due both to the transmission
measurement and sample non 1-D expansion, and the back-
light ununiformity, though estimated to be relatively less im-
portant, still remain to be resolved.
